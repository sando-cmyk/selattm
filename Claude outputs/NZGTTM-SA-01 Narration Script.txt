Welcome to TTM Site Assurance Assessor, a Sela Civil Advisory course. You already know how to set up and run a temporary traffic management site. This course is about the next step: standing back from a site you didn't build, judging whether it really protects the people working in it and travelling through it, and acting on what you find. There are six modules. Each one ends with a short knowledge check, and there's a final assessment at the end. SLIDE 2  |  Module 1  |  Why Assurance Exists
File: audio/m1/2.mp3   (~116 words, about 46 s)
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Module one: assurance under the NZGTTM. The New Zealand guide to temporary traffic management is risk-based. Instead of following a fixed layout, planners work out the real risks on each site and choose controls to suit. That gives flexibility, but it means the quality of a site now depends on judgement at every step, from the plan on paper to the last cone at night. Assurance is how the system checks that judgement. The guidance uses the Swiss cheese model. Every check has holes, but when you layer several different checks, a hazard is unlikely to get through all of them. Your assessment is one of those layers. It's important, but it isn't the whole system.

==========================================================================================
SLIDE 3  |  Module 1  |  The Six Assurance Processes
File: audio/m1/3.mp3   (~123 words, about 49 s)
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The NZGTTM guidance sets out six assurance processes, numbered A1 to A6. A1 is a systems audit of a company's management system. A2 is a TMP review, which checks the plan before it's built. A3 is a full risk-based review of the whole operation, done by a team of at least two. A4 is the STMS's own review of the site. A5 is a partial site condition review, which covers the G checks only. And A6 is the compliance-based site condition review, which compares what's built on site with the approved plan. Know which one you've been asked to do, and what it doesn't cover. A common mistake is treating a compliance review as proof that the plan itself is right. It isn't.

SLIDE 4  |  Module 1  |  Where You Fit
File: audio/m1/4.mp3   (~105 words, about 42 s)
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
So where does a Sela assessor fit? Most of the time you'll carry out partial reviews using the G checks, site condition reviews where the client or road controlling authority has accepted you as a reviewer, and Sela health and safety assurance visits, which add our priority checks and worker safety. You may also support an STMS review, or join a full risk-based review team under a lead reviewer. What you don't do is approve traffic management plans, redesign the site, or assess a site where you have a conflict of interest. You assess what's in front of you, act on immediate danger, and report.

SLIDE 5  |  Module 1  |  Your Duties
File: audio/m1/5.mp3   (~110 words, about 44 s)
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
When you walk onto someone else's site, you carry real duties. Under the Health and Safety at Work Act, every business on that site shares overlapping duties, and they must consult, cooperate and coordinate with each other. Not my site is never an option. Your own safety comes first. Follow the STMS's briefing and Sela's safe work method, because an assessor who gets hurt makes the site less safe. Stay independent. Declare any conflict of interest before the visit, and step aside if you need to. And be consistent. Two assessors looking at the same site should reach the same outcome, so use the form and the definitions every time.

SLIDE 7  |  Module 2  |  Twenty Years of Data
File: audio/m2/7.mp3   (~104 words, about 42 s)
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Module two: who gets hurt at our worksites. Sela analysed every crash reported in New Zealand from January two thousand and six to November twenty twenty-six. That's more than seven hundred thousand crashes. Fifteen thousand, five hundred and sixty-one of them were at worksites. They killed one hundred and fifty-eight people and seriously injured more than a thousand. Worksite crashes turn out to be about as severe as crashes in general. What has changed is how many there are. Before twenty sixteen, worksites made up about one point three per cent of all crashes. Since twenty seventeen, that's been three to four per cent.

==========================================================================================
SLIDE 8  |  Module 2  |  Open Roads
File: audio/m2/8.mp3   (~84 words, about 34 s)
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Here's the first big finding. Nine in ten worksite deaths happen at open-road sites. One hundred and forty-one of the one hundred and fifty-eight deaths were on open roads, and open-road local roads have the highest severity of any setting. Urban sites have plenty of crashes, but far fewer deaths. So when you assess an open-road site, spend your time on approach speeds, lead-in distances, whether drivers are actually slowing to the temporary speed limit, and how well workers are separated from live traffic.

==========================================================================================
SLIDE 9  |  Module 2  |  Darkness and Trucks
File: audio/m2/9.mp3   (~84 words, about 34 s)
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Two more things stand out: darkness and heavy vehicles. A third of worksite crashes happen in the dark, but they account for forty per cent of the deaths, and almost all of those night-time deaths were on open roads. Trucks are involved in about ten per cent of worksite crashes, but twenty-eight per cent of the fatal ones. Weather, interestingly, plays very little part. So night and after-hours visibility, queue-end protection, and heavy-vehicle stopping distances all deserve a proper look, not just a glance.

==========================================================================================
SLIDE 10  |  Module 2  |  Cyclists and Pedestrians
File: audio/m2/10.mp3   (~103 words, about 41 s)
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
What about cyclists and pedestrians? When a motorcyclist, cyclist or pedestrian is involved in a worksite crash, twenty to thirty-one per cent of those crashes are fatal or serious. For cars, it's about five per cent. But crashes involving a cyclist or pedestrian make up only six per cent of worksite deaths. Two cautions, though. Crash counts don't measure exposure, so pushing cyclists into a live lane is high risk for every person who passes. And falls on plate edges, ramp lips and loose chip rarely reach police records. So their provision stays non-negotiable, even though most of the death toll sits elsewhere.

==========================================================================================
SLIDE 11  |  Module 2  |  TSL, Not the Tick
File: audio/m2/11.mp3   (~83 words, about 33 s)
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Here's something every assessor should know. Police tick the road works box in only six point seven per cent of worksite crashes. If you rely on that box alone, you'll miss about ninety-two per cent of worksite deaths. The most reliable marker is the temporary speed limit recorded in the crash. So whenever you look up crash history for a site, or read a client's safety case, check that worksite crashes were identified by temporary speed limit, not just the road works tick.

SLIDE 12  |  Module 2  |  Sela Priority Checks
File: audio/m2/12.mp3   (~78 words, about 31 s)
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Sela turns these findings into five priority checks, P1 to P5. They sit alongside the G checks on every assessment. P1 is the open-road approach. P2 is night and after-hours visibility. P3 is heavy vehicles and queues. P4 is the cyclist and pedestrian route. And P5 is surface hazards, like plate edges, ramp lips and loose chip. You won't give every check the same time on every site. Match your effort to the site in front of you.

SLIDE 14  |  Module 3  |  Compliance vs Risk
File: audio/m3/14.mp3   (~85 words, about 34 s)
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Module three: risk-based thinking. On every site, there are two questions. The first is compliance: is the site built the way the traffic management plan says? The second is risk: does what's built actually protect people, here and today? A site can match its plan exactly and still be unsafe, because the plan missed something, or because conditions have changed. That's where G7 comes in. It asks whether the plan suits the actual environment, workspace, activity and hazards. It's the bridge between compliance and risk.

==========================================================================================
SLIDE 15  |  Module 3  |  Hierarchy of Controls
File: audio/m3/15.mp3   (~102 words, about 41 s)
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The hierarchy of controls ranks how effective a control is. At the top is elimination, like detouring traffic away from the work altogether. Then substitution, like portable traffic signals instead of a person at the roadside. Then isolation, such as rated barriers. Then engineering controls, such as a truck-mounted attenuator. Below those are administrative controls, like signs, speed limits and safe work methods. And at the bottom is personal protective equipment, the last line of defence. If a higher control wasn't used, the reason should be recorded. On site, ask the STMS why, and note it if that reason no longer holds.

==========================================================================================
SLIDE 16  |  Module 3  |  Testing G7
File: audio/m3/16.mp3   (~89 words, about 36 s)
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Here are some questions to ask on every site to test G7. What has changed since the plan was written? Is the workspace what the plan assumed? Where would a distracted driver end up, and what would they hit? Where does the queue reach at peak, and can approaching drivers see it in time? How would a wheelchair user, a child or a cyclist get past? And what's left behind when the crew goes home? Stand where a road user would be, and picture the tired or speeding driver.

SLIDE 18  |  Module 4  |  Before You Go
File: audio/m4/18.mp3   (~95 words, about 38 s)
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Module four: conducting the site assessment. Good assessments start before you leave the office. Get the traffic management plan, the approval and the diagrams, because you can only check G5, G7 and G8 against what was approved. Check the site risk rating and speed limit approval. Look at previous reports and any open corrective actions. Check local crash history, using temporary speed limit crashes. Confirm you have no conflict of interest. Pack your PPE, beacon, camera or dashcam, and the assessment form. And make sure you have the STMS's number and the after-hours on-call number.

==========================================================================================
SLIDE 19  |  Module 4  |  On Arrival
File: audio/m4/19.mp3   (~111 words, about 44 s)
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
When you arrive, follow five steps. First, drive through the site in both directions where it's safe, just as a road user would, ideally recording on dashcam. Second, park safely, clear of the traffic path and the working space, and never inside the taper. Third, if the site's active, find the STMS, introduce yourself and get the site safety briefing. Fourth, get approval from the person supervising the working space before you enter it. And fifth, check the paperwork: the plan on site, the approval, the on-site record, and the STMS's qualification. The drive-through matters, because it shows you the site as a driver sees it, before anyone knows you're there.

==========================================================================================
SLIDE 20  |  Module 4  |  The G Checks
File: audio/m4/20.mp3   (~99 words, about 40 s)
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Complete all eight G checks on every assessment. G1 checks the STMS is qualified for the site. G2 checks the temporary speed limit is appropriate and has integrity, with a sign at every change. G3 checks traffic has its own clear path. G4 checks the on-site record is complete and accurate. G5 checks an approved plan exists. G6 checks the plan is on site. G7 checks the plan suits the real conditions. And G8 checks the layout matches the approval. On an active site, a failed G1, G2, G3 or G5 means a stop work order until it's fixed.

==========================================================================================
SLIDE 21  |  Module 4  |  Walking the Site
File: audio/m4/21.mp3   (~101 words, about 40 s)
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Walk the site from approach to exit, looking at it from both the road user's view and the worker's view. In the advance warning area, can drivers see the first sign in time for their speed? In the transition, are the taper and arrow board right, and is the speed limit at the change? In the buffer and work area, is the safety zone clear, and are workers and plant inside the workspace? Is the traffic path wide, smooth and well marked? Is the pedestrian and cyclist route continuous and safe? And at termination, are drivers returned safely to normal conditions?

==========================================================================================
SLIDE 22  |  Module 4  |  Evidence
File: audio/m4/22.mp3   (~89 words, about 36 s)
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Your report may be read by the STMS, the client, the road controlling authority, WorkSafe, or even a court. So your evidence has to stand up. Every photo should be time-stamped and located. Take a wide shot for context, then a close-up of the defect. Number your photos and refer to them in your findings. Write down who told you what, and when. Avoid photographing faces unless you need to. And never stand in the traffic path to get a photo. Use a dashcam or a longer lens instead.

==========================================================================================
SLIDE 23  |  Module 4  |  Night and Unattended Sites
File: audio/m4/23.mp3   (~97 words, about 39 s)
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Forty per cent of worksite deaths happen in the dark, nearly all on open roads. So after-hours checks are core assurance, not an optional extra. At night, check visibility and speed limit integrity in depth. Is everything that was left out still complete, standing, lit and visible? If you find a hazard on an unattended site, phone the traffic management contractor's on-call contact to fix it straight away. Record it with time-stamped photos. If you don't get a prompt response, escalate to the client and the road controlling authority. Don't leave a dangerous site without raising it.

SLIDE 25  |  Module 5  |  Site Outcomes
File: audio/m5/25.mp3   (~83 words, about 33 s)
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Module five: outcomes and immediate action. The NZGTTM guidance gives three site outcomes. High standard or acceptable means the site matches the approved plan and controls the risk. You give feedback and record good practice. Unacceptable means there are deficiencies that reduce protection, but no immediate danger. You supervise improvements on the spot and confirm the fix. Dangerous means there's an immediate risk of serious harm. You supervise immediate improvements or issue a stop work order, and a corrective action plan is required.

==========================================================================================
SLIDE 26  |  Module 5  |  Rating Findings
File: audio/m5/26.mp3   (~93 words, about 37 s)
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Individual findings are rated too. An observation is good practice, or an opportunity to improve with no non-conformance. A non-conformance is where the site differs from the plan, the guide or the contract in a way that reduces protection. A critical finding is an immediate risk of serious harm. One critical finding makes the site dangerous. One or more non-conformances, without a critical finding, generally makes it unacceptable. Scoring won't always capture the overall feel of a site, and the guidance allows your judgement to override it. Just make sure you record why.

==========================================================================================
SLIDE 27  |  Module 5  |  Stop Work Orders
File: audio/m5/27.mp3   (~99 words, about 40 s)
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
On an active site, a failed G1, G2, G3 or G5 means a stop work order until it's resolved. That's an unqualified person in charge, a speed limit that's wrong or missing, traffic without its own clear path, or no approved plan. Other failures go to the STMS or their company for a prompt fix, and if they're not fixed, you escalate. When you issue a stop work order, tell the STMS calmly which check failed and what needs to happen. Record the time. Notify the client and Sela operations. Then confirm the fix, and record when work restarts.

==========================================================================================
SLIDE 28  |  Module 5  |  The Conversation
File: audio/m5/28.mp3   (~85 words, about 34 s)
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The conversation with the STMS matters as much as the checklist. Start with what's working well. There's usually something. Be specific about where, what, and why it matters. Agree the fix and who'll do it, and watch it done where you can. Stay calm and factual. You're not there to win an argument. You're there to make the site safe. Explain how they can appeal if they disagree. And if things get heated or you feel threatened, step away, stay safe, and call Sela operations.

SLIDE 30  |  Module 6  |  Anatomy of a Finding
File: audio/m6/30.mp3   (~83 words, about 33 s)
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Module six: reporting and close-out. Every finding follows the same pattern, so anyone can understand it without having been there. Where and when: the exact location and time. What you saw: the facts, with photo numbers. Why it matters: who could be hurt, and how. The requirement: what the plan, approval or guide requires, and which check it relates to. The action: what was done, by whom, and when. And the rating. Ask yourself: could someone who wasn't there understand exactly what happened?

SLIDE 31  |  Module 6  |  Submitting the Report
File: audio/m6/31.mp3   (~71 words, about 28 s)
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Complete the Sela site assurance assessment form on the platform, and attach your numbered photos. Submit it within twenty-four hours of the visit. If the site was rated dangerous, or you issued a stop work order, phone it through to Sela operations and the client the same day. Your first ten reports will be peer-checked by a Sela lead assessor. Your report goes to the client and to the STMS's company.

==========================================================================================
SLIDE 32  |  Module 6  |  Corrective Action and Close-out
File: audio/m6/32.mp3   (~80 words, about 32 s)
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Every dangerous outcome needs a corrective action plan, with the root cause, the fix, an owner and a date. Close-out has to be verified, not assumed. Check it with photos, a follow-up visit, or a later sample review. Be wary of a corrective action that only says toolbox talk held. Ask what has actually changed. And when you see the same issue across several sites, that's a trend. Report it to the client. That's how assurance prevents the next crash.
