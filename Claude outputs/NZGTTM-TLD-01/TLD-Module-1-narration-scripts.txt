Module 1 – Design Speed and Reaction Time - narration scripts

=== Slide 1: Welcome  ->  slide1.m4a  (141 words, about 60 s) ===
Kia ora, and welcome to TMP Layouts and Dimensions. In the risk assessment course you learned how to identify risks and choose controls. This course is about the next step: turning that plan into a layout with the right distances. Every number on a traffic management diagram, from sign spacing to taper length, comes from how long people need to see something, decide what to do, and act, at the speed they're travelling. Once you understand where the numbers come from, you can check them, explain them, and change them with confidence when your risk assessment says you should. There are six modules: design speed and reaction time, signs, exclusion zones, tapers and delineation, sight distance and mobile operations, and finally drawing and checking your diagram. You'll finish with a worked lane closure and a final assessment. Have a calculator handy.

=== Slide 2: Where the Numbers Come From  ->  slide2.m4a  (151 words, about 65 s) ===
Where do the numbers come from? Every dimension in the NZGTTM table is a time multiplied by a speed. A driver needs time to see a sign, understand it and act on it. A worker needs time to get out of the way. A vehicle needs time and distance to stop. Multiply that time by the speed of the traffic and you get a distance. That's why the NZGTTM table gives both: a distance in metres, and a time in seconds. The time lets someone on site check a set-out without a measuring wheel. Watch a fast vehicle travel between two points and count the seconds. The table values are a starting point, not a rule. You're expected to risk-assess every site and activity. That might mean making a distance longer, or sometimes shorter. Either way, you now have a technical reason for the change, and you should write it down.

=== Slide 3: Design Speed  ->  slide3.m4a  (160 words, about 69 s) ===
Which speed should you design to? You have two options. The permanent speed limit is easy to find on the National Speed Limit Register. The operating speed is the 85th percentile speed, the speed that 85 percent of drivers travel at or below. That is the better measure of risk, but you have to survey enough vehicles to get it. Use the permanent limit for the approach to the site, where drivers haven't yet slowed down. Once traffic has slowed, for example inside a static site, you can design to a lower operating speed. But there's a catch. If you design to a lower speed, you must make sure that speed is actually achieved. A temporary speed limit sign on its own often isn't enough. Narrower lanes, thresholds, manual control or a pilot vehicle might be what makes the speed real. On site, the time values in the table let you adjust set-out to the speed traffic is really doing.

=== Slide 4: Activity: Reaction Time  ->  slide4.m4a  (134 words, about 57 s) ===
Reaction time is really four steps, called PIEV. Perception: you notice something ahead. Identification: you work out it's a cone, a sign or a worker. Emotion: how you feel about it. Volition: you decide to act. Only then do you brake or steer. The NZGTTM assumes reaction time grows with speed, because lower-speed roads tend to be busier and more complex, which keeps drivers more alert. So there are three groups: 1.5 seconds for 30 to 50 kilometres an hour, 2 seconds for 60 to 80, and 2.5 seconds for 90 to 110. These three groups come up again and again in this course. For people on foot, the NZGTTM uses a conservative walking speed of 1.2 metres per second. Now try the activity. Tap each site to see which speed you'd design to.
