Module 3 – Exclusion Zones - narration scripts
Record one file per slide, named as shown (slideN.m4a or .mp3). Read at a natural pace; about 130-150 words per minute.

=== Slide 10: Longitudinal Exclusion Zone  ->  slide10.m4a  (144 words, about 62 s) ===
Module three: exclusion zones. The longitudinal exclusion zone is the empty space between the end of the taper and the start of the work area. It's there to absorb mistakes. If a driver misses the taper and runs into the closed lane, this is the room they have to stop before they reach people or plant. The NZGTTM bases it on stopping sight distance, the distance to see a low object and brake to a stop. It uses a shorter reaction time here, 1.5 seconds for most speeds and 2 seconds at a hundred and a hundred and ten, because by now drivers have passed the signs and should be alert. The deceleration rate for sealed roads is 0.36, the desirable rate from Austroads, which is a little conservative to allow for trucks. Keep this zone empty: no parked vehicles, no materials, no workers.

=== Slide 11: Sealed, Unsealed and Low Friction  ->  slide11.m4a  (116 words, about 50 s) ===
Not every site has a good sealed surface. Gravel, a road under construction, oil, loose chip, soil, steel plates over a trench, or ice all make stopping longer. For unsealed surfaces the NZGTTM uses a deceleration rate of 0.25. At eighty kilometres an hour that turns about a hundred and three metres on a good seal into about a hundred and thirty-four metres on gravel. The table can't cover every surface, so you're expected to judge the friction and extend the zone when you need to. The same goes for traffic with lots of heavy vehicles. Trucks take longer to stop than cars, so on a freight route consider a longer zone than the table gives.

=== Slide 12: Lateral Exclusion Zone  ->  slide12.m4a  (128 words, about 55 s) ===
The lateral exclusion zone is the sideways buffer between the line of cones and the work area. It allows for a driver who drifts out of their lane. The NZGTTM assumes a gentle drift of 0.6 metres per second over the reaction time, which gives about 0.9 metres in the 30 to 50 group, 1.2 metres at 60 to 80, and 1.5 metres at 90 to 110. But a driver who swerves will cross that zone much faster, and won't necessarily stop at the edge of your work area. So ask: what's on the other side? Fresh seal at the same level is one thing. Workers on foot, a trench, or a parked excavator is another. The greater the hazard beyond the zone, the wider it should be.

=== Slide 13: Excavations and Barriers  ->  slide13.m4a  (135 words, about 58 s) ===
Excavations need extra width. The NZGTTM uses an angle of repose approach: widen the lateral exclusion zone by two units for every unit of depth. So a trench five hundred millimetres deep adds one metre. A trench eight hundred millimetres deep adds 1.6 metres. That keeps the edge of the trench from collapsing under a vehicle and gives a drifting wheel somewhere to go. If you can't fit that width, or the excavation is deep, the risk is high and a temporary road safety barrier should be on the table. Remember from the risk assessment course: barriers sit high in the hierarchy of controls, because they physically stop the vehicle. But a barrier needs its own deflection space, the right end treatments and an installation designer. So it's a design decision, not just a swap.

=== Slide 14: Activity: Size the Zone  ->  slide14.m4a  (54 words, about 23 s) ===
Time to size some zones. For each site, think about the longitudinal zone, the lateral zone, and anything that should make them bigger. Then tap to check your thinking. Remember to use the NZGTTM table values on a real TMP. These calculations show you where those values come from, and why you'd change them.
