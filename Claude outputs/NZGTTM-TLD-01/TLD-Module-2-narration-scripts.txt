Module 2 – Signs: Visibility, Spacing and Warning Distance - narration scripts
Record one file per slide, named as shown (slideN.m4a or .mp3). Read at a natural pace; about 130-150 words per minute.

=== Slide 5: Sign Visibility Distance  ->  slide5.m4a  (134 words, about 57 s) ===
Module two: signs. A sign only works if drivers can see it in time. Sign visibility distance is the distance over which a driver must be able to see the sign before reaching it. The NZGTTM sets it at one and a half times reaction time. That fifty percent margin gives the driver time to read the sign and decide what to do. At fifty kilometres an hour that's about thirty-one metres. At eighty it's about sixty-seven metres. At a hundred, about a hundred and four. This applies to every sign, whether it's on a stand or on a vehicle. A tail pilot vehicle parked clear of the live lane is really just a sign on wheels. On your diagram, check that nothing blocks that view: a crest, a curve, parked trucks or vegetation.

=== Slide 6: Sign Spacing and Complex Signs  ->  slide6.m4a  (120 words, about 51 s) ===
Sign spacing is the distance between one sign and the next. Drivers need at least their reaction time to respond to each sign before the next one arrives. The NZGTTM adds a twenty-five percent margin, so sign spacing is one and a quarter times reaction time. At fifty kilometres an hour that's about twenty-six metres. At eighty, about fifty-six metres. At a hundred, about eighty-seven metres. The standard values assume a simple message, like the temporary warning signs in the Traffic Control Devices Rule. A variable message sign with several words takes longer to read. So when a sign carries more information, give it more visibility distance and more spacing. Keep messages short, and don't stack too many signs together.

=== Slide 7: Warning Distance  ->  slide7.m4a  (153 words, about 66 s) ===
Warning distance is the total distance from the first advance warning sign to the start of the taper, or the start of the closure. It's the length of road a driver has to react in. For a stop/go, it's the distance in which they have to stop. The NZGTTM works it out from safe intersection sight distance, or SISD: the distance a driver needs to see a hazard and stop before it. Because the driver is warned from the moment they see the first sign, warning distance is SISD minus the sign visibility distance. At eighty kilometres an hour SISD is about a hundred and eighty-one metres, so warning distance is about a hundred and fourteen. The calculated value works out between 1.7 and 2.3 times the sign spacing, so the NZGTTM's rule of thumb is to allow at least twice the sign spacing. If your diagram has less than that, look again.

=== Slide 8: Queues and the Approach  ->  slide8.m4a  (143 words, about 61 s) ===
Your warning distance only works if drivers meet the signs before they meet the back of a queue. If traffic can queue past your first sign, drivers will come round a corner at full speed and find stationary cars, with no warning at all. So think about queue length. How much traffic, how long will you hold it, and how often? If the queue could reach the advance warning, move the signs further back, or add a queue warning sign. The NZGTTM design guides call this out directly: increase sign spacing to allow for queue lengths. Also check the approach itself. A sign placed just past a crest or on the inside of a curve may meet the spacing on paper, but fail the visibility test on the road. Drive or walk the approach, or use street imagery, before you finalise the diagram.

=== Slide 9: Activity: Pick a Speed  ->  slide9.m4a  (98 words, about 42 s) ===
Let's put module two together. Tap each speed to see its reaction time, sign visibility, sign spacing and warning distance. Notice how the numbers jump between the groups. Going from fifty to eighty kilometres an hour roughly doubles the warning distance, because both the speed and the reaction time go up. Try working each one out yourself before you tap: reaction time times speed in metres per second, then times one and a half for visibility, and times one and a quarter for spacing. To convert kilometres an hour to metres per second, divide by three point six.
