=== Slide 15: Taper Length  ->  slide15.m4a  (131 words, about 56 s) ===
Module four: tapers, lanes and delineation. A taper moves traffic sideways out of a closed lane. What matters to the driver is how fast they're being moved sideways, the lateral shift rate. The NZGTTM uses one metre per second. So to shift a 3.5 metre lane, the driver needs 3.5 seconds of travel. At eighty kilometres an hour that's about seventy-eight metres. At fifty, about forty-nine. At a hundred, about ninety-seven. Why one metre per second, when right-turn bays use 0.6? Because a TTM taper comes with advance warning and tall devices, and a gentler taper just makes the site longer and encourages people to keep their speed. These lengths can be used on gentle curves too, as long as the curve is above the minimum radius. We'll get to that.

=== Slide 16: Distance Between Tapers  ->  slide16.m4a  (102 words, about 44 s) ===
When one taper follows another, traffic needs time to settle before the next shift. The NZGTTM sets the minimum distance between tapers as the longitudinal exclusion zone for a sealed road. That's about a hundred and three metres at eighty kilometres an hour. Think of an alternating flow site on a two-lane road. There's a taper into the opposing lane, a run alongside the work area, then a taper back. If the second taper comes too soon, drivers are still adjusting from the first, and a mistake is much more likely. Lane changes, merges and contraflow crossovers all need the same thinking.

=== Slide 17: Temporary Lane Width  ->  slide17.m4a  (128 words, about 55 s) ===
How wide should a temporary lane be? There's no single answer, and the NZGTTM says so. A lane has to be wide enough for every vehicle that will use it, and narrow enough to keep speeds down. Here's the problem with the old 2.75 metre figure. The maximum legal width of a heavy vehicle is 2.55 metres. That leaves just 100 millimetres either side, and that's before mirrors, which can stick out another 240 millimetres. On a curve, trucks need even more width because of their swept path. So look at the vehicle mix, the curves and gradient, cyclists, how the lane is delineated, and what else is keeping speeds down. Narrow lanes also mean more cones get hit, and that sends workers back out to fix them.

=== Slide 18: Delineation on Straights  ->  slide18.m4a  (135 words, about 58 s) ===
Delineation along the work area has one job: make it obvious that drivers mustn't pass through the line. Two things matter: how many devices drivers pass each second, and whether the gaps look too small to drive through. The NZGTTM groups the spacing with the reaction times: five metres for 30 to 50, ten metres for 60 to 80, and fifteen metres for 90 to 110. Here's the key point. The slower the traffic, the easier it is to steer between cones. So if traffic is actually slower than the limit, close the spacing up. On a hundred kilometre road where traffic through the site is doing seventy, use ten metres, not fifteen. And keep it simple for the crew. If fifteen metres will be hard to judge on site, specify ten. Don't specify twenty.

=== Slide 19: Tapers, Curves and Thresholds  ->  slide19.m4a  (152 words, about 65 s) ===
In a taper, or on a tight curve, the line of devices appears across the driver's path. It needs to look like a wall. With 400 millimetre cones and a 3.5 metre lane, that takes about ten devices. The NZGTTM sets taper spacing so crews can use the road markings instead of a tape measure. At 30 to 50 kilometres an hour, 2.5 metres: five devices from the start of one dashed line to the start of the next. At 60 to 80, five metres: three devices. At 90 to 110, ten metres: one at the start of each dash. If you're using flexible delineators, which are narrower than cones, close the spacing up. Thresholds are five devices at the same spacing as the taper, either side of the approach, to make the site feel narrower and slow drivers down. Your risk assessment may call for more devices or a longer threshold.

=== Slide 20: Activity: Tapers on Curves  ->  slide20.m4a  (138 words, about 59 s) ===
Curves change everything. Driving round a curve is already a sideways movement. The NZGTTM shows that the radius at which a curve gives about a one metre shift is roughly V squared divided by twenty-six. That's about a hundred metres at fifty, two hundred and fifty at eighty, and three hundred and ninety at a hundred. If you put a taper to the right on a right-hand curve, the two add together, and drivers get close to two metres per second. Desirably, don't put tapers on curves. If you must, don't put a taper that tightens the curve on any curve below that radius, unless the curve taper has specific geometric design. You can estimate a radius from aerial imagery in the office. On site it's hard to measure safely, so generic TMPs need care. Tap each case.
