=== Slide 21: Clear Sight Distance for Workers on Foot  ->  slide21.m4a  (160 words, about 69 s) ===
Module five. When workers are on foot on a live road, they need enough warning to get off it before a vehicle arrives. That's clear sight distance. The NZGTTM measured it two ways. First, getting out of a parked vehicle: checking the mirror, unbuckling, getting out, closing the door, and walking to a safe spot took ten and a half to twelve seconds. Second, walking off the road: two seconds to react, three and a half seconds to pick up a tool like a measuring wheel, and about six and a half seconds to walk up to eight metres across two lanes at 1.2 metres per second. That's about twelve seconds again. So clear sight distance is twelve seconds of travel. At eighty kilometres an hour that's about two hundred and sixty-five metres. The old three times the speed limit rule only gave about eleven seconds, so it's no longer used. Bigger vehicles, slower people, or longer tasks need more.

=== Slide 22: Mobile Operations  ->  slide22.m4a  (192 words, about 82 s) ===
Mobile operations move, so their dimensions are in seconds. The tail pilot, parked clear of the live lane, is really a sign on a vehicle, so it needs sign visibility distance, not clear sight distance. That gives you more freedom to position it. But if it's too far behind the work vehicle, drivers react, see nothing, and go back to normal. So the gap from tail pilot to work vehicle should be at least the warning distance and no more than safe intersection sight distance. In time, that's three to six seconds at 30 to 50 kilometres an hour, four to eight seconds at 60 to 80, and five to ten seconds at 90 to 110. Move the tail pilot up whenever you can. The gap between the shadow vehicle and the work vehicle has no single answer. The old ten metres doesn't account for a truck hitting a TMA, which can push it fifty metres or more. Consider the traffic mix, the TMA, both speeds, and whether anyone should be on foot in that gap at all. Where sight distance is poor, ask whether a mobile operation is the right method.

=== Slide 23: The Design Guide Diagrams  ->  slide23.m4a  (136 words, about 58 s) ===
The NZGTTM includes eight design guide diagrams, adapted from Austroads. They show the four basic ways to manage traffic around a site. Go round the site: a closure and detour, design guides one and two. Go through the site: contraflow or signals, design guide three. Go past the site: lane closures and shoulder work, design guides four, five, six and eight. And in the gaps: short stops between traffic, design guide seven. Each one comes with NZGTTM notes, risk considerations, designer considerations and STMS considerations. For example: will over-height vehicles fit the detour? How will construction vehicles get in and out without traffic following them? What happens if the road can't reopen before the peak? The guides are prompts for your thinking. They are not traffic management plans and must not be used for operational work.

=== Slide 24: Activity: Which Method?  ->  slide24.m4a  (42 words, about 18 s) ===
Which method fits each job? Think about the risk, the duration, the traffic and the space. Then tap each card. There's often more than one reasonable answer. What matters is that you've considered the higher-order options first, and can explain your choice.
