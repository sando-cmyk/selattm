3. How a Hazard Causes Harm: The Causal Pathway

~5 min read

Being able to name a hazard is not the same as being able to explain how it causes harm — and Standard 40233 is explicit that the second skill is what's being taught. Every hazard in this module follows the same three-step causal pathway:

Hazard — the source of potential harm (a vehicle, a piece of plant, an overhead line, a person)

Mechanism — the specific way that source actually reaches and affects a person (a vehicle breaches the TTM boundary, an excavator boom swings through the six-metre airspace, a driver loses patience and leaves their vehicle)

Harm — the resulting injury, illness, or psychological impact (a struck-by injury, an electric shock, a physical or psychological assault)

Using this pathway is what turns ‘traffic is dangerous’ into something you can actually act on: ‘a driver who doesn't understand our signage may not slow down before the taper, may not see the worker standing at the limit line, and may strike them’ tells you exactly what control is missing — clearer signage, better positioning, or both.

Every hazard covered in the Hazard Spotting activity that follows is written out using this same hazard → mechanism → harm structure. Get comfortable with it here, because you'll be asked to apply it, not just recite it.

Example: an approaching vehicle (hazard) → a driver misses the signage (mechanism) → a struck-by injury (harm).

By the end of this section you can:

  • Describe the three-step causal pathway: hazard, mechanism, harm
  • Explain why identifying the mechanism matters more than just naming the hazard
KEY IDEA

Key idea: ‘What could go wrong?’ is only half the question. The half that actually protects someone is ‘how would it go wrong, specifically, right here?’ — that's the mechanism step, and it's the one people skip.
