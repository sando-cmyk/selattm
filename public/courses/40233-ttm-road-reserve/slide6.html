4. Hazard Spotting: Six Common Onsite Hazards

~12 min read (interactive)

1. Live traffic and vehicle movements

Hazard: an approaching or passing vehicle. Mechanism: a driver misreads, doesn't see, or chooses to ignore TTM signage and taper, and the vehicle breaches the TTM boundary before it has slowed — or, less often, a driver deliberately drives at a worker. Harm: a struck-by injury, ranging from minor to fatal. This is the highest-consequence hazard in TTM work, precisely because the source of harm never actually stops moving.

2. Moving plant and equipment

Hazard: mobile plant working within or crossing the TTM zone (trucks, excavators, rollers, reversing vehicles). Mechanism: a plant operator has a blind spot, a worker is in an unmarked or unenforced exclusion zone, or there's no dedicated spotter for a reversing manoeuvre. Harm: a struck-by or crush injury.

3. Overhead hazards

Hazard: anything occupying or intruding into the road reserve's six-metre airspace — power lines, structures, a raised truck body, a crane or excavator boom. Mechanism: plant is operated without checking overhead clearance, or a load is raised without accounting for what's above it. Harm: electric shock or electrocution, or a struck-by injury from a falling or swinging load.

4. Vulnerable road users

Hazard: pedestrians, cyclists, people with disabilities, and other vulnerable road users sharing the space around a worksite. Mechanism: a temporary path or crossing is unfamiliar, poorly surfaced, or not clearly separated from a vehicle access point — a real 2019 fatality on a Christchurch motorway project happened exactly this way: a truck turned into a site driveway across a temporary pedestrian pathway and struck a cyclist, in part because the TMP's required minimum usable shoulder for cyclists wasn't being maintained. Harm: a struck-by injury, often severe, because vulnerable road users have the least protection of anyone in or near the TTM zone.

5. Environmental and site conditions

Hazard: reduced visibility or traction — night conditions, glare, inclement weather, obscuring vegetation, a degraded road surface. Mechanism: a driver's sight distance to the worksite, its signage, or its workers is compromised, so they don't have time to react even if they're paying attention. Harm: a collision with a worker, plant, or TTM device, or a loss-of-control incident.

6. Aggression, fatigue and human factors

Hazard: a frustrated or aggressive road user, or a fatigued worker. Mechanism: a driver delayed by the worksite verbally abuses, throws an object at, or physically confronts a worker — or a worker whose concentration has degraded from a long shift misses an approaching hazard. Harm: physical or psychological injury to a worker, or an incident caused by reduced worker alertness.

1. Live traffic & vehicle movements

Mechanism: A driver misreads, doesn't see, or ignores TTM signage and taper, and the vehicle breaches the TTM boundary before it has slowed.

Harm: A struck-by injury, ranging from minor to fatal — the highest-consequence hazard in TTM work.

Control: Correctly spaced, clean TTM signage and taper, positioned so an inattentive driver still has time to react.

2. Moving plant & equipment

Mechanism: A plant operator has a blind spot, or a worker is in an unmarked or unenforced exclusion zone.

Harm: A struck-by or crush injury.

Control: A dedicated spotter for reversing manoeuvres, and a physically marked, enforced exclusion zone.

3. Overhead hazards

Mechanism: Plant, a raised load, or an overhead service intrudes into the road reserve's six-metre air space without clearance being checked.

Harm: Electric shock or electrocution, or a struck-by injury from a falling or swinging load.

Control: A checked overhead clearance before any plant or load is raised, and a documented safe approach distance to live power lines.

4. Vulnerable road users

Mechanism: A temporary path or crossing is unfamiliar, poorly surfaced, or not clearly separated from a vehicle access point.

Harm: A struck-by injury, often severe — vulnerable road users have the least protection of anyone near the TTM zone.

Control: A clearly separated, appropriately surfaced temporary path or crossing matching the TMP's minimum clearances.

Real incident: 30 Oct 2019: a cyclist was fatally struck by a concrete truck turning into a Christchurch worksite across a temporary pedestrian pathway — the site had failed to maintain the minimum one-metre usable shoulder for cyclists required by its own TMP.

5. Environmental & site conditions

Mechanism: A driver's sight distance to the worksite, its signage, or its workers is compromised by night conditions, glare, weather, vegetation, or a degraded surface.

Harm: A collision with a worker, plant, or TTM device, or a loss-of-control incident.

Control: Lighting, signage retroreflectivity, and speed management appropriate to actual conditions, reviewed as conditions change.

6. Aggression, fatigue & human factors

Mechanism: A driver delayed by the worksite verbally abuses, throws an object at, or physically confronts a worker — or a fatigued worker's concentration lapses.

Harm: Physical or psychological injury to a worker, or an incident caused by reduced alertness.

Control: A clear process for reporting abusive road user behaviour, and fatigue management built into the roster.

CASE STUDY

Real incident: on 30 October 2019, a cyclist was fatally struck by a concrete truck turning into a Christchurch motorway worksite, across a temporary pedestrian pathway. WorkSafe found the site had failed to maintain the minimum one-metre usable shoulder for cyclists required by its own TMP, and hadn't provided adequate information, instruction, and supervision on traffic management. The resulting Enforceable Undertaking funded the industry's first dedicated Practice Note on protecting vulnerable road users in TTM environments — proof that the causal pathway in hazard 4 above isn't theoretical.
