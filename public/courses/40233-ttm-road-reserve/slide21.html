3. Communicating With Your Crew & Supervisor

~7 min read (interactive)

Learning Outcome 1c requires that information is communicated with TTM workers and the supervisor within the road reserve, following TMP and organisational requirements. In practice, that breaks down into a few concrete habits:

Say what changed, not just that something changed. ‘The rain's picked up’ is less useful than ‘visibility on the northern taper has dropped, I'd say to about 40 metres.’

Use your radio, and keep it within reach — clear, direct communication is a safety tool, not paperwork. Regular updates, not just emergency calls.

Know your organisation's critical notifications list — the specific events (any injury, a vehicle breaching the site, equipment failure, traffic disruption above a set level) that must be reported to your supervisor immediately, not at the end of shift.

Document what you did and why. Recording a change you made — what, when, and why — means your supervisor, the next shift, and even the TMP designer can learn from it. A photo or a short narrated video often communicates a site condition faster and more clearly than a written description.

Communication isn't a separate task alongside the physical work — it's part of doing the physical work safely.

Read the scenario, then choose the response you'd actually radio through.

Scenario 1

Visibility on your northern taper has just dropped because of sudden rain. You need to radio your supervisor. What do you say?

Too vague — your supervisor will need to ask follow-up questions, costing time.
Buries the important information — your supervisor has to dig for the key point.
Specific and actionable — your supervisor can respond immediately with a clear picture of the problem.

Scenario 2

A member of the public's vehicle just clipped a cone and kept going — a near-miss with no injury. What do you do?

Too slow — this belongs on your organisation's critical notifications list, reported immediately.
Correct — a vehicle breaching the site is a critical notification, reported straight away, not saved for later.
The near-miss itself is what matters, whether or not anything was damaged.

By the end of this section you can:

  • Describe what “critical notifications” are and why they're reported immediately
  • Explain why documenting a change (not just making it) matters
NOTE

Your phone's camera is one of your most useful communication tools on site. A short video, narrated as you walk through what changed, is often faster and clearer than a written report — and easier for your supervisor to act on.
