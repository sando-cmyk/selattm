2. The Hierarchy of Controls & Lowest Total Risk

~7 min read

Every control on a TTM site — cones, signage, a reduced speed limit, a spotter, PPE — comes from somewhere. The Health and Safety at Work Regulations 2016 set out a hierarchy of controls, in order of effectiveness:

Eliminate — remove the hazard entirely (e.g. close the road so there's no interaction with traffic at all)

Substitute — replace a higher-risk activity with a lower-risk one (e.g. a different construction method)

Isolate — physically separate people from the hazard (e.g. a barrier system)

Engineering — reduce the hazard through design (e.g. speed-reducing chicanes, extra warning signage)

Administrative — manage the hazard through process (e.g. only trained workers near live traffic)

PPE — personal protective equipment as the last line of defence (e.g. high-visibility clothing)

Controls lower on this list — administrative and PPE — don't physically separate anyone from the hazard, so they're the least effective on their own, even though they're often the easiest to add.

Waka Kotahi's guidance also introduces lowest total risk: reducing one risk can increase another, and a good decision weighs the total picture, not just the risk directly in front of you. A worked example from the NZGTTM guide: closing a road eliminates risk to workers, but if the only detour is 360 kilometres and five hours longer, that's a large risk transferred to the travelling public — total risk may not actually go down. The better answer in that real case was a short, well-timed closure with periodic openings — an eliminate-tier control that avoided transferring an unacceptable amount of risk elsewhere.

The hierarchy of controls, Health and Safety at Work Regulations 2016 — most effective at the top.

By the end of this section you can:

  • List the hierarchy of controls in order, from most to least effective
  • Explain the concept of lowest total risk, and why reducing one risk can increase another
CASE STUDY

Real example: replacing a culvert on a narrow, undetoured stretch of State Highway 6 near Nelson. A full road closure eliminated risk to workers — but the only detour added 360km and five hours to the public's journey. The lowest-total-risk answer was a closure with periodic openings: workers protected while closed, minimal extra delay for road users. Total risk, weighed across everyone affected, not just the crew.
