Why It Happens, and the Rules

~2 min (interactive) · narrated

Why it happens

  • Wrong side: drivers can’t see the barrier arm or lights
  • Stop/go near a crossing confuses people
  • Queues stack back over the tracks
  • Plant, fencing and sign clutter block sight lines
  • Most STMSs and TCs have no rail experience

The rules

  • Within 100 m of a crossing: TMP (and changes) approved by KiwiRail
  • Within 10 m of the track: notify KiwiRail
  • Within 5 m of the crossing or track: KiwiRail Permit to Enter
  • Prefer a road closure over changing how people use the crossing

Stop just past the tracks

Unsafe. The queue stacks back over the crossing. A held car can end up stopped on the tracks.

Stop before the crossing

Better, but a driver given “go” can still meet the alarms starting – the Waikato near miss. Needs KiwiRail approval (within 100 m), coordination with crossing signals, and possibly rail protection.

Close the road (if practicable)

Often the safest. Nobody uses the crossing in a new way. It still needs KiwiRail approval and a planned detour.

Why does this happen? On the wrong side of a crossing, drivers can't see the barrier arm or the flashing lights. Stop/go near a crossing confuses people. Queues from your site can stack back over the tracks. Plant, fencing and sign clutter block sight lines to trains and signals. And most STMSs and traffic controllers have no rail experience. So here are the rules. Within a hundred metres of a level crossing, your TMP, and any change to it, must be approved by KiwiRail. If your TTM comes within five metres of the crossing or the track, you need a KiwiRail Permit to Enter. Work or TTM within ten metres of the track must be notified to KiwiRail. When a crossing is directly affected, consider closing the road rather than changing how people use the crossing, and keep pedestrians using the crossing as designed.
