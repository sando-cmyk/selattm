Survivable Speeds: the Safe System

~1 min · narrated

New Zealand’s road safety approach is built on the Safe System. It accepts that people make mistakes, and designs the system so that a mistake doesn’t cost a life. It’s based on the impact speeds the human body can usually survive:

Crash typeSurvivable impact speed (approx.)Why
Person on foot struck (worker, pedestrian, cyclist)≈ 30 km/hNo structure absorbs the energy. The body takes the full stop.
Side impact, vehicle into vehicle≈ 50 km/hLittle crush space between the occupant and the striking vehicle.
Head-on, vehicle into vehicle≈ 70 km/hCrumple zones, belts and airbags extend the stop.
A ROAD WORKER IS AN UNPROTECTED ROAD USER

Above about 30 km/h, survival is unlikely. On most worksites live traffic is passing well above that, so separation, not luck, has to protect the crew.

New Zealand's approach to road safety is called the Safe System. It accepts that people make mistakes, and it designs roads and worksites so that a mistake doesn't cost someone their life. It's built on the impact speeds the human body can usually survive. For a person on foot, that's about thirty kilometres an hour. For a side impact between vehicles, about fifty. For a head-on, about seventy, because crumple zones, belts and airbags stretch out the stop. A road worker is an unprotected road user. There's no crumple zone around you. Above about thirty, survival is unlikely, and most traffic past a worksite is travelling well above that. So separation has to protect the crew.
