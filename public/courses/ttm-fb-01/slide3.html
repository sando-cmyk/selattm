Occupant Injury Potential

~1 min · narrated

When a vehicle hits a fixed object there is an energy transfer: the vehicle slows or stops. Modern vehicles push some of that energy into deformation through crumple zones. But the occupant keeps going at the same velocity until something stops them. So do the body’s organs.

1Vehicle collision

The vehicle hits the signpost and crushes. The crumple zone slows it over about half a metre.

2Human collision

The occupant keeps moving until the seatbelt stops them, or, without a belt, the steering wheel, dash or windscreen.

3Internal collision

The brain, heart, liver and aorta keep moving and strike the inside of the skull, rib cage and abdomen.

Consider hitting a signpost at 100 km/h

≈ 65 gCar’s average deceleration (0.6 m crush)
≈ 45 gBelted occupant: rides down with the car
700+ gUnbelted occupant: hits the interior after the car has stopped
WHY SEATBELTS WORK

The belt starts slowing you early, so you use the car’s crumple zone too. Without it, you hit the interior at the full 100 km/h and stop in centimetres. Wear a belt in every site vehicle, every time.

Let's look at what happens to the people inside a vehicle. When a vehicle hits a fixed object, there's an energy transfer and the vehicle slows or stops. Modern vehicles push some of that energy into crumple zones. But the occupant keeps going at the same speed until something stops them: the seatbelt, or without one, the steering wheel or windscreen. Then the organs keep moving too, until they hit the inside of the body. That's three collisions in one crash. Consider hitting a signpost at a hundred kilometres an hour. A belted occupant slows down with the car, at roughly forty-five g. An unbelted occupant hits the interior at full speed after the car has already stopped, at hundreds of g. That's the difference a seatbelt makes.
